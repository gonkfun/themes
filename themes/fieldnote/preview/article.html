A house sparrow learns the feeder

Three mornings of patience, one sunflower seed and a lesson in watching without hurrying.

Iris Moreau · fictional demo authorObserved Mar 14Back gardenClear, 6°C

The feeder went up on a Monday. By Wednesday the blue tits had found it, but the sparrows kept to the hedge, chattering about it from a safe distance.

Margin noteSparrows often feed in groups. A single bird approaching first is worth recording: note the time.

Day one: the hedge.

Six birds, all in the hawthorn. Every few minutes one would drop to the lawn beneath the feeder, pick at spilled seed and flick back up. None landed on the feeder itself.

Watching without hurrying is the whole skill. The bird decides when the story starts.

Day two: the first landing.

At 07:42 a male with a dark bib landed on the perch, took one sunflower seed and left. Thirty seconds later, two more followed.

KitA pencil, a small notebook and a watch. Binoculars help but aren’t needed at a feeder.

Day three: the regulars.

By the third morning the sparrows came straight from the hedge to the feeder. The routine had changed in two days, and the notebook is the only reason I noticed.

  • Write the date, time and weather first.
  • Count before you describe.
  • Note what changed since yesterday.
